06 · Analysis — how difficulty drives time and energy
P(win per hash) = 16−d E[attempts] = 16d E[time] = 16d / H E[energy] = Pwatt · 16d / H E[cost] = energy × price

Time and energy share one exponential curve. The proof-of-work check — hash < target — is a lottery each attempt wins with probability 16−d. So expected attempts, expected time and expected energy all multiply by ×16 with every single difficulty step. Energy doesn't get its own curve: for fixed hardware it is literally power × time, which is why the calibration dots you mined in section 04 sit on a straight line in log-space, and why the energy table in section 05 is the same table with different units. The bar you watched grind at difficulty 6 in this browser is difficulty 7 costing sixteen times more for one more zero.

Difficulty adjustment converts speed into consumption. Retargeting (toggle it in the deck) exists to hold block time constant: when hashrate joins the network, blocks arrive early, difficulty rises, and the block interval returns to target — but energy per block is now permanently higher. Nothing else changed; the target simply chased the machines. This is the feedback loop you can watch oscillate live when you add or remove workers in retarget mode.

Scale check against reality. Bitcoin's current target works out to roughly 78 leading zero bits (~19.5 hex zeros, ~3×10²³ expected hashes per block). One device mining solo would expect to wait — per block; the real network finds one every ~10 minutes because on the order of — such machines race in parallel, drawing ≈— and consuming ≈— per year. Difficulty didn't create that energy use on its own — it is the ratchet that makes hardware progress spend itself.
